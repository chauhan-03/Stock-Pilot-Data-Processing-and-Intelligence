1. WHAT THIS PROJECT IS, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
================================================================================

ReorderIQ is a simulated "inventory control tower" - the kind of system a
grocery chain's operations team would use to answer "which stores are about
to run short on which products, and what should we do about it" - built on
top of the real Instacart Market Basket dataset (3.42 million real grocery
orders). Instacart's data has no stores, warehouses, or stock levels in it,
so the project is split cleanly into two halves: a demand layer built
entirely on real transactions (a machine learning model that predicts what
people will reorder, and market-basket rules about what gets bought
together), and a network layer that had to be invented from scratch (18
simulated "stores" clustered out of real shoppers, an inventory math layer on
top of them, and an exception-flagging system that ties it all together).
Every output is labeled as one or the other, because pretending simulated
numbers are real is the single most common way these portfolio projects lose
credibility in an interview.


================================================================================
2. THE CORE PROBLEM: WHAT INSTACART'S DATA DOES AND DOESN'T GIVE YOU
================================================================================

Instacart's public dataset (originally released for a Kaggle competition)
records real shopping behavior: which products real users bought, in what
order, in which orders, and how many days passed between orders. This is
genuinely rich data for demand modeling.

What it does NOT contain:
  - Any store, location, or warehouse identifier. Every order just belongs to
    a user - there's no sense of "this order happened at Store #4."
  - Any real calendar dates. You only get the number of days since a user's
    previous order (a gap), never an actual date.
  - Any stock levels, inventory counts, or supplier/lead-time information.
  - A usable held-out test set. Kaggle split each user's most recent order
    into "train" (labels visible) or "test" (labels hidden forever, for
    leaderboard scoring only) - the "test" portion is permanently unusable
    for anyone outside the original competition.

An "inventory control tower" is fundamentally about stores, warehouses, and
stock levels - none of which exist here. So this project does the honest
thing: it builds those missing pieces on top of the real data, as clearly
labeled simulations, rather than either (a) faking a slicker dataset or (b)
pretending the missing pieces don't matter. Section 13 is the full ledger of
which numbers in this project are real and which are constructed.


================================================================================
3. THE DATASET, EXPLAINED
================================================================================

Six CSV files, all from the Instacart Market Basket Analysis Kaggle
competition:

  orders.csv (3,421,083 rows)
    One row per order: which user placed it, whether it's part of that
    user's "prior" history or their held-out "train"/"test" order, what
    number order this was for that user (order_number), the day of week and
    hour it was placed, and days_since_prior_order (the gap since their last
    order - blank for a user's very first order).

  order_products__prior.csv + order_products__train.csv (33,819,106 rows
  combined once joined to orders)
    One row per (order, product): which products were in that order, what
    position they were added to the cart in, and whether that product counts
    as a "reorder" (the user had bought it before) or a first-time purchase.

  products.csv (49,688 rows), aisles.csv (134 rows), departments.csv (21 rows)
    The product catalog and its two-level category taxonomy (department is
    the broad category like "produce" or "dairy eggs"; aisle is more
    specific, like "packaged vegetables fruits").

  206,209 distinct users appear across the data.

Every downstream number in this project traces back to these six files and
nothing else - no external data was added.

4. CONCEPT: REORDER PREDICTION (THE DEMAND-SENSING MODEL)
================================================================================

WHAT IT PREDICTS
  For every user, and for every product that user has bought before, predict
  whether that product will appear in the user's NEXT order. This is
  deliberately scoped as a "reorder" model, not a general purchase-prediction
  model - it only ever considers products a user has already bought at least
  once, which is exactly how Kaggle's own competition was scored and keeps
  the whole exercise honest (you can't reasonably predict someone will buy
  something you've never seen them show interest in).

WHY IT MATTERS TO THE REST OF THE PROJECT
  This model is the "Demand Sensing" layer - the real, forward-looking signal
  that the rest of the system (store demand rollups, safety stock, and later
  the control tower's exception logic) is built on top of.

HOW IT WORKS
  For every (user, product) pair the user has bought before, 16 features are
  computed using ONLY that user's order history up to (but not including)
  their final held-out order:
    - User features: how many orders they've placed, their average basket
      size, how many days typically pass between their orders, their overall
      reorder ratio, how many distinct products they've ever bought.
    - Product features: how often the product is ordered overall, its
      overall reorder rate, its typical position in the cart.
    - User x Product interaction features (the strongest signal): how many
      times this specific user bought this specific product, how recently,
      what fraction of their orders included it, and their reorder rate for
      it specifically.
  These features feed a gradient-boosted decision tree classifier (scikit-
  learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier - architecturally the same family of
  algorithm as the more famous LightGBM/XGBoost, swapped in because this
  build machine has no Homebrew/libomp, which LightGBM's compiled library
  needs to load).

HOW IT'S EVALUATED (AND WHY THAT'S HARDER THAN IT SOUNDS)
  Kaggle's real competition metric isn't a simple accuracy score - it's mean
  F1 PER ORDER. For every held-out order, you have to predict a whole SET of
  products (which ones will reappear), and you're scored on how well that
  predicted set matches the true set, then averaged across all orders. This
  is harder than scoring individual predictions because it requires picking a
  probability cutoff (above what probability do you say "yes, they'll
  reorder this") that works well across an entire order's worth of products
  at once, not just one row at a time.
  This project tunes a single global cutoff (0.18) on a held-out slice of
  USERS (not rows) - 13,120 users set aside entirely, so their orders were
  never seen during training - and evaluates mean per-order F1 on them. This
  is the standard simplified version of what Kaggle's top solutions do (they
  use a slightly more complex per-order-optimal cutoff for a small extra
  gain).

  LEAKAGE CHECK (important): the split is done by USER, not by row. If you
  split by row instead, some of a user's other (user, product) pairs could
  leak into the training set while their held-out order's rows go to
  validation - the model could partially "learn" that user's specific
  patterns from the training side. Splitting by user means the validation
  users are entirely unseen.

RESULT
  Mean per-order F1: 0.3706, against a Kaggle public-leaderboard range of
  0.38-0.40 - reached with only 16 hand-built features and no hyperparameter
  tuning. Validation AUC: 0.831.

5. CONCEPT: SYNTHETIC STORE CLUSTERING (BUILDING THE MISSING NETWORK)
================================================================================

WHY THIS EXISTS
  As covered in Section 2, Instacart has no store dimension at all. To build
  anything resembling a "network" of stores that could each run short on
  different products, one has to be invented. The key design principle: the
  clusters must be built from real behavioral signal (not random assignment),
  and their validity as distinct "stores" must be checked statistically, not
  just assumed.

HOW IT'S BUILT
  Every one of the 206,209 users gets a profile:
    - Their department purchase-share: out of everything they've ever
      bought, what fraction went to produce, what fraction to dairy, snacks,
      beverages, etc. (21 numbers per user, one per department, summing to
      100%). This is a decent proxy for "what kind of shopper is this" -
      someone who's 40% produce shops very differently from someone who's
      40% snacks.
    - Shopping cadence: how many orders they've placed, their average days
      between orders, their typical day-of-week and hour, and their average
      basket size.
  These profiles are standardized (put on comparable scales) and clustered
  using K-means, a standard unsupervised clustering algorithm that groups
  similar profiles together into a fixed number of groups. Each resulting
  cluster becomes one "synthetic store" - the users in it are the shoppers
  that store notionally serves.

CHOOSING HOW MANY STORES (K)
  K-means requires you to pick the number of clusters up front. Four
  candidate counts (12, 15, 18, 20) were compared using silhouette score (a
  measure of how well-separated the clusters are), and 18 was chosen as a
  reasonable middle ground between having enough network granularity to be
  interesting and keeping each store's sample size large enough to be
  statistically meaningful.

VALIDATING THE CLUSTERS ACTUALLY MEAN SOMETHING
  This is the step that separates "defensible simulation" from "made-up
  numbers." Two checks were run:
    - Silhouette score: came out low (0.05-0.08). This is EXPECTED and
      stated openly - behavioral/purchase-share clustering doesn't produce
      the tight, well-separated geometric clusters that, say, clustering
      people by physical location would. A low silhouette score alone would
      be a red flag if it were the only check.
    - Kruskal-Wallis test (a statistical test for whether multiple groups
      have different distributions) run independently for each of the 21
      departments' purchase share, comparing all 18 stores against each
      other. Result: 21 out of 21 departments differ significantly across
      the 18 stores (p < 0.01 for every single one). A "pseudo-F statistic"
      (comparing between-cluster variance to within-cluster variance) of
      9,397 confirms this isn't a marginal effect.
  Conclusion: even though the clusters aren't geometrically tight, they are
  statistically and meaningfully distinct in what departments their shoppers
  favor - which is exactly the property a "store" needs to have for
  downstream inventory logic to make sense. Store sizes end up ranging from
  798 to 35,588 users - some imbalance is expected and fine, since nothing
  forced equal-sized clusters.

6. CONCEPT: HIERARCHICAL DEMAND FORECASTING AND RECONCILIATION
================================================================================

THE MISSING-CALENDAR PROBLEM
  To forecast "next week's demand" for a store-product, you need a notion of
  weeks. Instacart gives no real dates - only days_since_prior_order, the gap
  before each order. This project reconstructs a RELATIVE timeline: for each
  user, starting their very first order at day 0, it cumulatively adds up
  each subsequent gap to get a running day count, then buckets that into
  7-day weeks. This recovers each user's own order spacing accurately, but
  does NOT align users to a shared calendar - "week 3" for one user is not
  the same real-world week as "week 3" for another. That's fine for this
  project's purpose, because forecasts are built per store-product using that
  store's own demand history, not by comparing calendar weeks across stores.

THE HIERARCHY
  Once every order-product row has a store (from the clustering in Section
  5) and a relative week, demand can be counted at the SKU level (product),
  rolled up to the department level (treated as a "category DC" -
  distribution center - serving the whole network for that category), rolled
  up further to the store level, and finally to the whole network. This
  four-level hierarchy - SKU -> department-DC -> store -> network - is what
  lets later layers ask questions at whichever level makes sense (a single
  product at one store, or a whole category's demand network-wide).

  For tractability, this hierarchy is built over the top 3,000 products by
  order volume (out of 49,688 total), which still covers 73% of all
  order-product volume - a reasonable trade of completeness for a
  computation that stays fast.

FORECASTING NEXT WEEK'S DEMAND
  For each (store, product) combination, a weighted moving average over its
  last 6 observed weeks of demand (more recent weeks weighted more heavily)
  produces a forecast for the next week. This is a deliberately simple,
  transparent forecasting method - the point of this project isn't to build
  the most sophisticated time-series model, it's to have a believable demand
  number to feed the inventory math in Section 7.

RECONCILIATION (WHY THE NUMBERS ADD UP)
  "Bottom-up reconciliation" means: forecast at the most granular level
  (store x product), then simply SUM upward to get store totals, department
  totals, and the network total. Because it's addition, the totals are
  guaranteed to match exactly at every level - there's no risk of a
  department's total forecast disagreeing with the sum of its stores'
  forecasts, which is a real problem in more complex ("top-down" or
  "middle-out") reconciliation approaches. This was verified explicitly: the
  SKU-level total, the sum via store rollup, and the sum via department
  rollup all matched to the last decimal.

7. CONCEPT: MEIO - SAFETY STOCK, REORDER POINTS, AND RISK POOLING
================================================================================

WHAT MEIO MEANS
  MEIO = Multi-Echelon Inventory Optimization. "Multi-echelon" means there's
  more than one tier in the supply chain being optimized together (here:
  stores, and the department-level DC above them), rather than optimizing
  each location in isolation. The "optimization" is about deciding how much
  buffer stock (safety stock) to hold at each tier and where to hold it, to
  hit a target service level as cheaply as possible in total inventory.

THE BASIC INVENTORY MATH (PLAIN LANGUAGE)
  - Safety stock is extra inventory held beyond expected demand, to absorb
    the fact that real demand varies week to week. The more demand varies
    (higher standard deviation) and the longer it takes to get more stock
    (lead time), the more safety stock you need.
  - Reorder point (ROP) is the inventory level at which you should trigger a
    new order - it's roughly "how much you'll sell during the time it takes
    to get more stock" plus the safety stock buffer.
  - Service level is the target probability of NOT running out before the
    next delivery arrives. This project targets 95%, a common retail
    default, which corresponds to a statistical "z-score" of about 1.645 (a
    standard number from the normal distribution used to size the buffer).

THE FORMULA, IN THE FORM ACTUALLY USED
    safety_stock = z * demand_std_dev * sqrt(lead_time / period_length)
    reorder_point = average_daily_demand * lead_time + safety_stock

THE TWO ECHELONS HERE
  - Store level (downstream): each store holds its own safety stock, sized
    against ITS OWN demand variability for that product, with a fast 1-day
    replenishment lead time from the DC (a stated assumption - Instacart
    provides no real lead-time data).
  - Department-DC level (upstream): rather than just adding up every store's
    safety stock, the DC's buffer is sized against the POOLED demand
    variability across all the stores it serves, with a slower 3-day
    replenishment lead time from the supplier (also a stated assumption).

WHY POOLING SAVES INVENTORY (THE ACTUAL INSIGHT)
  This is the classic "risk pooling" principle from supply chain theory:
  when you combine several independent, variable demand streams into one
  pool, the COMBINED variability (as a percentage of the combined average) is
  smaller than any single stream's variability on its own. Concretely: if
  each of 10 stores has some random ups and downs in demand, some of those
  swings cancel each other out when you look at all 10 together, so the
  DC serving all 10 doesn't need nearly as much safety stock as the sum of
  what each store would need if buffering completely on its own. This
  project computes both numbers explicitly to make the benefit visible: if
  every store held its own full safety stock independently, the network
  would need 190,832 units. Pooling that variability at the DC echelon
  instead needs only 133,325 units - a 30.1% reduction in total network
  safety stock, for the same 95% service level target.

  IMPORTANT CAVEAT (stated honestly): this calculation assumes each store's
  demand is statistically independent of the others. In the real world,
  regional demand is often correlated (a heat wave affects many nearby
  stores at once), which would shrink this 30.1% benefit somewhat. It's
  still a directionally correct and standard textbook argument - just not a
  number to treat as precise to the decimal.

8. CONCEPT: MARKET BASKET / ASSOCIATION RULE MINING
================================================================================

WHAT IT ANSWERS
  Two related questions: "what products tend to get bought together"
  (useful for bundling, recommendations, and - critically for this project -
  figuring out ripple effects of a stockout), and "what products substitute
  for each other" (useful for recommending an alternative when the primary
  item is running short).

KEY TERMS
  - Support: how often a pair of products appears together, as a fraction of
    all orders.
  - Confidence: given that product A was bought, what fraction of the time
    was product B also bought.
  - Lift: how much more (or less) often A and B appear together than you'd
    expect if they were bought completely independently of each other. Lift
    of 1 means no relationship; lift much greater than 1 means a strong
    positive association (complements); lift well below 1 means the two are
    bought together LESS than chance would predict (often substitutes -
    people buy one OR the other, not both).

TWO METHODS USED HERE, DELIBERATELY DIFFERENT IN SCOPE
  1. Exact pairwise co-occurrence, computed via a direct database self-join
     across the FULL 33.8-million-row order history (top 2,000 products by
     volume, to keep the computation bounded) - 415,479 candidate pairs
     scored with exact, non-sampled numbers. This is the primary,
     trustworthy output:
       - "Frequently bought together" (complements): pairs with lift > 1.5
         and reasonable confidence. Top example: Coconut Chocolate Bar and
         Chocolate Sea Salt (bar), lift 329x.
       - "Substitute candidates": pairs from the SAME AISLE (so genuinely
         comparable products) with lift below 0.8 - shoppers picking one
         instead of the other. Example: Bag of Organic Bananas and Banana,
         lift 0.02x (people overwhelmingly buy one or the other, essentially
         never both in the same order, which makes complete intuitive
         sense). 700 such substitute pairs were found.
  2. A genuine FP-Growth run (a well-known frequent-itemset-mining
     algorithm, via the mlxtend library) on a 106,310-order SAMPLE of the
     top 250 products. FP-Growth can find patterns involving THREE OR MORE
     items at once, which the pairwise method above can't see. Sampling here
     is a hard memory-tractability requirement - encoding the full 3.42
     million orders x 49,688 products as the one-hot matrix FP-Growth needs
     is not feasible on this machine - so this method is explicitly
     secondary and only used for the "beyond pairs" multi-item view, never
     for the primary substitute/complement logic above. Result: 1,475
     frequent itemsets at a 0.3% minimum-support threshold.

9. CONCEPT: THE CONTROL TOWER (EXCEPTION DETECTION)
================================================================================

WHY IT'S NOT AN LLM/AI-AGENT SYSTEM
  Every decision the control tower makes - is this store-product combination
  an exception worth flagging, which other store could send inventory to
  cover it, which product could substitute for it - is fully computable
  directly from the data with clear, deterministic logic. There's no
  ambiguous judgment call here that an LLM would improve on, so this layer
  is built as a plain rule-based Python orchestrator instead of an AI agent
  framework (like LangGraph). This keeps it fast, free to run, fully
  testable, and with no dependency on an API key. A thin LLM layer could
  sit on top purely to turn the output into natural-language summaries for a
  human reader, but that would be a presentation layer, not a decision-maker
  - noted as a possible future addition, not something the system needs.

THE HARD PART: DEFINING "EXCEPTION" SO IT MEANS SOMETHING
  The first, naive idea was: flag a store-product if the forecasted demand
  (Section 6) is unusually high compared to that same store-product's own
  historical average and standard deviation. This turned out to be a dead
  end - the forecast IS built from that same historical average (it's a
  moving average of recent weeks), so comparing it back against its own
  history rarely disagrees with itself. That approach flagged essentially
  zero real exceptions.

  The fix: compare two INDEPENDENT estimates of demand instead of comparing
  one number to itself.
    - Estimate 1: the historical trailing-average baseline from Section 6
      (what MEIO's safety stock was actually sized against).
    - Estimate 2: the trained reorder-prediction model from Section 4,
      applied fresh to every user in a store and summed up - a genuinely
      forward-looking, independently-derived demand signal based on real
      behavioral features (recency, frequency, cart position), not on the
      trailing average at all.
  These two numbers live on different scales (one is "expected units per
  week," the other is closer to "expected units in a user's next order
  cycle"), so instead of comparing raw values, each store-product's SHARE of
  its store's total demand is compared under each method. This cancels out
  the scale mismatch and turns the question into something operationally
  meaningful: is the ML model predicting a meaningfully different demand MIX
  than the trailing average would suggest?

  A second lesson learned the hard way: the very first version of this share
  comparison used raw ratio thresholds and flagged 58.6% of all store-product
  positions as "exceptions" - which is not a signal, that's just noise
  dressed up as insight (if the majority of everything is an "exception,"
  the word has stopped meaning anything). Tightening the definition (a
  1.5x/0.67x share-ratio threshold, plus a minimum absolute-volume floor to
  ignore tiny low-traffic SKUs where noise dominates) brought the exception
  rate down to a much more credible 4.9% - a rate that a human operations
  team could actually act on.

WHAT HAPPENS ONCE SOMETHING IS FLAGGED
  For each flagged exception:
    - If it looks like understock risk (ML demand share meaningfully above
      the historical baseline share): the system searches every OTHER store
      carrying the same product for one with spare capacity (its own
      forecasted demand well below what it's stocked for) and recommends it
      as a transfer donor, picking whichever has the largest surplus.
    - Regardless of direction, the system looks up the substitution rules
      from Section 8 for that product and surfaces the closest substitute
      (same aisle, lowest lift) as a fallback recommendation.
  Final result: 2,397 exceptions out of 49,256 store-SKU positions (4.9%),
  of which 1,079 have a same-network transfer donor available and 821 have a
  substitute product recommendation attached.

10. THE FULL END-TO-END FLOW, STEP BY STEP
================================================================================

This is the exact order the pipeline runs in (see run_pipeline.sh), and what
each step consumes and produces. Everything reads from and writes to a
single DuckDB database file at data/processed/reorderiq.duckdb, so each step
builds directly on tables the previous step created.

  STEP 1 - Ingest (src/reorderiq/ingest/build_db.py)
    Reads the six raw CSVs from data/raw/ and loads them into DuckDB as
    typed tables: orders, order_products, products, aisles, departments, and
    a pre-joined fact_order_products table (33.8M rows) that combines all of
    them for fast downstream querying. Indexes are built on the columns
    everything else joins on.

  STEP 2 - Build reorder features (src/reorderiq/features/build_features.py)
    Computes the 16 user/product/interaction features described in Section
    4, strictly from eval_set='prior' rows only (never touching the held-out
    order), and assembles the final labeled training dataset: 8,474,661
    candidate (user, product) rows across 131,209 users, 9.8% positive.

  STEP 3 - Train the reorder model
    (src/reorderiq/models/train_reorder_model.py)
    Splits users 90/10, trains the gradient-boosted classifier, tunes the
    probability threshold on the held-out 10% to maximize mean per-order F1,
    and saves the trained model plus a metrics report. Produces:
    data/processed/reorder_model.joblib and
    reports/reorder_model_metrics.json.

  STEP 4 - Cluster synthetic stores
    (src/reorderiq/features/build_synthetic_stores.py)
    Builds the per-user department-share + cadence profile described in
    Section 5, runs K-means with k=18, validates the clusters with
    Kruskal-Wallis, and writes a user_store_map table (which user belongs to
    which of the 18 stores) into the DuckDB file, plus
    reports/store_clustering_validation.json.

  STEP 5 - Build the demand hierarchy
    (src/reorderiq/features/build_hierarchical_demand.py)
    Reconstructs the relative per-user calendar, buckets into weeks, builds
    the store x product x week demand table for the top 3,000 products, and
    computes the weighted-moving-average next-week forecast per
    (store, product), verifying bottom-up reconciliation holds exactly.

  STEP 6 - Compute MEIO (src/reorderiq/meio/build_meio.py)
    Reads the forecasts from Step 5, computes store-level safety stock and
    reorder points, computes the pooled department-DC-level safety stock
    across all stores serving each product, and reports the network-wide
    pooling benefit. Produces reports/meio_summary.json.

  STEP 7 - Mine association rules
    (src/reorderiq/rules/build_association_rules.py)
    Computes the exact full-history pairwise co-occurrence rules (Section 8,
    method 1) and runs the sampled FP-Growth pass (method 2), saving
    complement rules, substitute candidates, and multi-item rules as CSVs in
    reports/.

  STEP 8 - Run the control tower
    (src/reorderiq/agents/control_tower.py)
    Scores every candidate (user, product) row from Step 2 with the trained
    model from Step 3, rolls those probabilities up to store-level ML demand,
    compares against the historical baseline from Step 5 (as described in
    Section 9), flags exceptions, attaches transfer and substitute
    recommendations, and writes reports/control_tower_exceptions.json and
    reports/control_tower_summary.json.

  STEP 9 - Consolidate for the dashboard
    (src/reorderiq/dashboard_data.py)
    Pulls every report generated above into a single
    reports/dashboard_data.json file, which the HTML dashboard reads and
    renders as charts and tables.

The whole sequence is automated by run_pipeline.sh at the project root - it
runs all nine steps in this exact order with one command.

12. RESULTS SUMMARY (ALL NUMBERS IN ONE PLACE)

13. WHAT'S REAL VS. SIMULATED (READ THIS BEFORE TRUSTING ANY NUMBER)
================================================================================

REAL, UNMODIFIED:
  - All 3.42 million orders and 33.8 million order-product rows.
  - The reorder-prediction model and its evaluation (Section 4) - trained and
    scored entirely on real transactions, real labels.
  - The association rules (Section 8) - the pairwise rules are exact
    computations over the full real history, no simulation involved at all.

SIMULATED (CLEARLY LABELED, STATISTICALLY VALIDATED WHERE POSSIBLE):
  - The 18 "stores" themselves - genuine clusters of real users, but not real
    store locations or real store operations. Validated as behaviorally
    distinct (Section 5), not validated as geographically or operationally
    real, because they aren't.
  - The weekly calendar used for forecasting - reconstructed relative timing
    per user, not real synchronized calendar dates across users.
  - The 1-day store / 3-day DC lead times and the 95% service level target -
    stated modeling assumptions, since Instacart provides no supplier or
    fulfillment data whatsoever.
  - "Inventory levels" in general - there are no observed stock levels
    anywhere in the source data. Every inventory number in this project is a
    demand-coverage TARGET computed from forecasted demand and safety stock,
    not an observed shelf or warehouse count. This is stated as a modeling
    choice, not hidden.

CHECKED FOR CORRECTNESS:
  - No leakage into the held-out order: validation always uses a 90/10 split
    by USER (not by row), and the model is scored only against Kaggle's
    'train' eval_set orders (labels genuinely never seen), never the 'test'
    eval_set (which Kaggle permanently withholds labels for and which cannot
    be scored by anyone outside the original competition).

14. HOW TO REPRODUCE THIS YOURSELF
================================================================================

  1. Get the data: download the Instacart Market Basket Analysis dataset
     from Kaggle (kaggle.com/competitions/instacart-market-basket-analysis)
     - free login required, the dataset is no longer hosted anywhere else -
     and place the six CSVs in data/raw/.

  2. Set up the environment:
       python3 -m venv .venv
       source .venv/bin/activate
       pip install -r requirements.txt

  3. Run everything:
       ./run_pipeline.sh

  4. Open the results:
       dashboard/index.html          (interactive dashboard, open in a browser)
       docs/project-description.html (narrative write-up)
       reports/*.json, reports/*.csv (raw numbers behind everything)

15. GLOSSARY OF TERMS USED THROUGHOUT
================================================================================

AUC                 "Area Under the (ROC) Curve" - a score from 0.5 (no
                     better than guessing) to 1.0 (perfect) for how well a
                     model ranks positive cases above negative ones.

Confidence          In association rules: given product A was bought, the
                     fraction of the time product B was also bought.

DC                  Distribution center - a warehouse that supplies multiple
                     stores. Here, simulated at the department/category level.

F1 score            The harmonic mean of precision and recall - a single
                     number balancing "did I miss real reorders" against
                     "did I falsely predict reorders that didn't happen."

FP-Growth           An algorithm for finding frequent itemsets (products
                     that appear together often) in transaction data,
                     without having to check every possible combination.

K-means              A clustering algorithm that groups similar data points
                     into a fixed number (K) of groups, by repeatedly
                     assigning points to the nearest group center and
                     recomputing centers.

Kruskal-Wallis test A statistical test for whether several groups' value
                     distributions differ from each other significantly.

Lead time            How long it takes for a new order of stock to arrive
                     once triggered.

Lift                 In association rules: how much more/less often two
                     products appear together than pure chance would predict.
                     Lift 1 = no relationship, >1 = bought together more than
                     chance (complements), <1 = less than chance (often
                     substitutes).

MEIO                 Multi-Echelon Inventory Optimization - deciding safety
                     stock and reorder points across more than one linked
                     tier of a supply chain at once (e.g., stores and their
                     shared DC), rather than each location in isolation.

Reorder point (ROP)  The inventory level that should trigger placing a new
                     order for more stock.

Risk pooling          The supply-chain principle that combining several
                     independent variable-demand streams reduces the
                     COMBINED variability (as a share of total demand)
                     compared to each stream on its own - meaning less total
                     safety stock is needed when demand is aggregated.

Safety stock          Extra inventory held beyond expected average demand,
                     to absorb real-world demand variability without running
                     out.

Service level          The target probability of not running out of stock
                     before the next delivery arrives (this project targets
                     95%).

Silhouette score      A measure of how well-separated clustering results
                     are, from -1 (bad) to 1 (perfectly separated).

Support               In association rules: how often a pair of products
                     appears together, as a fraction of all orders.

z-score (in MEIO)     A statistical multiplier corresponding to a target
                     service level, used to size safety stock (95% service
                     level corresponds to z = 1.645).
